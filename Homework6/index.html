Riemann and Lebesgue Integration · Normal CDF

Numerical integration through domain and range partitions, and accurate computation of standard normal probabilities.

01Two approaches to integration

Riemann integration partitions the domain [a,b] into N intervals and sums f(xᵢ)Δx. We use midpoint samples for the principal numerical estimate.

IR,N = Σᵢ f(a + (i + ½)Δx) Δx,   Δx = (b − a)/N

Lebesgue integration groups points according to the values of f. For a nonnegative bounded function, the integral is approximated by value bins Bⱼ and the measures of their preimages.

IL,K ≈ Σⱼ yⱼ · λ({x ∈ [a,b] : f(x) ∈ Bⱼ})

In the browser, preimage measures are estimated using M uniform midpoint probes. This is a numerical range-binning approximation, not a symbolic computation of exact Lebesgue measures. K is the number of value bins; M is the number of domain probes. A common probe grid can cause sampling bias, particularly for pathological functions.

05Standard normal CDF: theory and method

For Z ~ N(0,1), the cumulative distribution function is the area under the Gaussian density to the left of z. Symmetry reduces the numerical work to an integral from 0 to |z|.

Φ(z) = (1/√(2π)) ∫−∞z exp(−t²/2) dt = ½[1 + erf(z/√2)]

Our main implementation uses composite Simpson's rule on [0,|z|] and the identity Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z). We also implement a power-series method to compare precision and cost. The displayed reference values are calculated from a high-precision reference table and independently checked against the NIST standard normal table.

∫₀ᵃ g(t)dt ≈ (h/3)[g(0) + g(a) + 4Σodd jg(jh) + 2Σeven jg(jh)]

Why not elementary functions? Liouville's theorem on elementary antiderivatives implies that exp(−x²) has no elementary antiderivative. This does not mean the integral does not exist: it is represented exactly by the special function erf, or by an infinite convergent series. Practical evaluation still requires algorithms that approximate erf or the integral to finite precision.

Φ(z) = ½ + (1/√(2π)) Σk≥0 [ (−1)k z2k+1 / (2k k! (2k+1)) ]

The uploaded Section 3.4 notes explain Gaussian normalization, the series antiderivative, symmetry, and the distinction between “no antiderivative” and “no elementary antiderivative.” For large |z|, direct alternating series terms can suffer cancellation; this demonstration restricts the interactive z range to [−4,4].

07Discussion and references

The numerical experiments show that Riemann and Lebesgue integration lead to the same result for Riemann-integrable functions, although their numerical approximations may converge at different rates. The examples involving discontinuous functions also illustrate why Lebesgue integration is more general.

For the normal distribution, Simpson's rule provides accurate results with relatively few evaluations, while the power series performs particularly well near zero. The results agree closely with the reference values, showing that both methods can be useful depending on the required accuracy and computational cost.

The main conclusion is that numerical methods provide practical ways to approximate quantities that may be difficult to calculate analytically. Choosing an appropriate method depends on the properties of the function and the desired precision.

References: The Normal Distribution — Hogg, McKean and Craig; NIST Standard Normal Distribution Table.
