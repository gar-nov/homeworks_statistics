Convergence and Bayesian Inference

An interactive statistical study of cryptocurrency transactions and fraud detection.

01Modes of convergence

Let Xₙ be a sequence of random variables and X its limit. Different modes of convergence describe different meanings of approximation as n increases.

ModeDefinition and interpretation
In probabilityP(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0. Large errors become unlikely.
Almost surelyP(lim Xₙ = X) = 1. Almost every entire sample path converges.
Mean square (L²)E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0. The expected squared error vanishes.
In distributionFₙ(t) → F(t) at every continuity point of F. The distributions converge, not necessarily the individual outcomes.

Almost sure convergence and L² convergence each imply convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution. The reverse implications generally fail. Convergence in distribution to a constant does imply convergence in probability.

For example, independent Bernoulli variables with P(Xₙ=1)=1/n converge to zero in L² and in probability, but not almost surely: successes occur infinitely often with probability one by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma. Conversely, Xₙ=n·1{U<1/n}, with U uniform on (0,1), converges almost surely to zero but has E[Xₙ²]=n. Finally, if X is a fair random sign and Xₙ=−X, the distributions agree but Xₙ does not converge to X in probability.

02Law of Large Numbers

Consider independent, identically distributed Bernoulli observations: Xᵢ=1 if a simulated cryptocurrency transaction is fraudulent and 0 otherwise, with probability p. The sample mean Mₙ=(X₁+…+Xₙ)/n estimates p. The weak LLN gives convergence in probability; the strong LLN gives almost sure convergence. Both hold for i.i.d. variables with finite absolute mean. Here finite variance also gives L² convergence, since E[(Mₙ−p)²]=p(1−p)/n.

Chebyshev's inequality gives P(|Mₙ−p|>ε) ≤ p(1−p)/(nε²). This bound tends to zero.

03The Central Limit Theorem and √n

For i.i.d. observations with mean μ and finite positive variance σ², the classical CLT states that √n(Mₙ−μ)/σ converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable. Unlike the LLN, the CLT describes the shape of the fluctuations around the mean.

Var(Sₙ) = nσ²   ·   Var(Mₙ) = σ²/n

In general Var(Sₙ)=Σ Var(Xᵢ)+2Σᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ). Independence implies zero covariance when second moments exist, so variances add; uncorrelatedness alone also suffices for this identity, but not for the classical CLT. The standard deviation of the sum grows as √n, while the standard error of the mean shrinks as 1/√n. Multiplication by √n therefore preserves a non-degenerate fluctuation scale. Dependent observations can have different variance and convergence behavior.

Histogram of standardized sample means; the curve is the standard normal density. For small n or rare events the approximation can be poor.

In statistical inference, the CLT motivates approximate confidence intervals Mₙ ± 1.96·s/√n and large-sample tests under suitable conditions. Increasing the sample size fourfold halves the standard error, not the bias. For Bernoulli data near 0 or 1, this simple normal interval can be unreliable.

Independent versus correlated transactions

Transactions from the same wallet or fraud campaign may share a risk factor. In an illustrative exchangeable Bernoulli model, with common fraud probability p and pairwise correlation ρ ≥ 0, Var(Mₙ) = p(1−p)[1+(n−1)ρ]/n. With positive fixed correlation, the variance does not vanish: the usual independent-sample LLN and √n scaling cannot simply be assumed.

The curves are exact model standard errors, not estimated market statistics. The simulation draws a common Bernoulli outcome with probability ρ for the entire sample; otherwise it draws independent outcomes. This produces the stated pairwise correlation.

04Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

In probability theory, P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B), provided P(B)>0. In statistics, conditional probabilities can be estimated from observed frequencies or specified by a statistical model. Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning direction:

P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B)

The prior P(A) represents uncertainty before observing evidence. The likelihood P(B|A) describes how probable the evidence is under the hypothesis. The posterior P(A|B) is the updated probability after observing the evidence. The denominator P(B) normalizes the result. A likelihood viewed as a function of a parameter is not generally a probability distribution over that parameter.

05Bayesian cryptocurrency fraud detection

Suppose A means a transaction is fraudulent and B means the detector raises an alert. Change the prior fraud rate, sensitivity and false-positive rate to see how the probability of an actual fraud changes after an alert. All values are hypothetical.

Even an accurate detector may generate mostly false alerts when fraud is rare. The posterior depends on the base rate as well as the detector's likelihoods. Incorrect or outdated rates can lead to misleading conclusions.

Repeated alerts and dependent evidence

Two alerts may come from independent detectors, or one may simply duplicate the other. For conditionally independent alerts, the joint likelihood is the product of the two likelihoods. For an exact duplicate, the second alert provides no new information. Conditional independence must hold under both fraud and legitimate-transaction hypotheses.

06Learning an unknown fraud rate

Instead of assuming a known fraud probability θ, assign a Beta(a,b) prior. For k fraudulent transactions among m independent Bernoulli observations, the binomial likelihood is proportional to θᵏ(1−θ)ᵐ⁻ᵏ. Bayes' theorem gives the posterior Beta(a+k,b+m−k). Its mean is (a+k)/(a+b+m).

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and observed proportion, with weights a+b and m. Strong priors have more influence with limited data. The posterior can become the prior for the next independent batch under the same model.

Prior sensitivity

Compare your selected prior with a uniform Beta(1,1) prior and a hypothetical skeptical Beta(1,19) prior, using the same observations. These priors are illustrative, not calibrated from blockchain records.

Bayesian inference incorporates previous knowledge and quantifies uncertainty about parameters. Its limitations include sensitivity to prior choice, model misspecification, dependence between observations and computational cost in complex models. More data can reduce prior influence in this model, but cannot repair systematic bias or an incorrect likelihood.

07Discussion and references

The LLN explains consistency of sample averages, whereas the CLT describes their sampling fluctuations. Bayesian inference addresses uncertainty by combining a prior distribution and a likelihood. These approaches are complementary: under suitable conditions, increasing independent evidence improves precision, but the conclusions depend on the assumptions of each model.
