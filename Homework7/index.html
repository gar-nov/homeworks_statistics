Statistical Independence

An interactive analysis of authentication methods and unauthorized login attempts using contingency tables and a chi-square test.

01Independence and association

Two random variables are independent in probability if knowing one does not change the probability distribution of the other: for every pair of values, P(X=x, F=f) = P(X=x)P(F=f). Equivalently, whenever P(F=f)>0, P(X=x | F=f) = P(X=x).

In statistics, independence is assessed from a finite sample. Sample frequencies rarely satisfy these equalities exactly, even when the underlying variables are independent. A chi-square test evaluates whether the observed differences are larger than would reasonably be expected from sampling variation.

02Dataset and contingency table

We use a synthetic cybersecurity dataset relating authentication method (X) to login outcome (F). The categories are Password only, SMS MFA and Authenticator app, with outcomes Unauthorized access or Access blocked. Each CSV row represents one simulated login attempt. Results update automatically as you edit the observations. The data are illustrative, not real-world incident records, and do not establish the effectiveness of any authentication technology.

04Expected frequencies and chi-square

Under the null hypothesis of independence, the expected count in cell (i, j) is Eᵢⱼ = (row totalᵢ × column totalⱼ) / n. The Pearson statistic compares observed counts O with expected counts E:

χ² = Σ (Oᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ

The degrees of freedom are (rows − 1)(columns − 1). Large values of χ² provide evidence against independence.

The p-value is computed using the chi-square distribution's upper-tail probability, evaluated through the regularized incomplete gamma function. Pearson's asymptotic approximation may be unreliable when expected counts are small. Cramér’s V summarizes association strength on a scale from 0 to 1.

05Interpretation

Association is not causation. Even if authentication method and login outcome are associated, these simulated data cannot prove that MFA caused a particular outcome. Attack difficulty, account protections, user behavior and how attempts were selected could all influence the results. Likewise, a non-significant test does not prove exact independence.

06Discussion and references

Contingency tables organize the joint distribution of two categorical variables. Marginal frequencies describe each variable separately, while conditional frequencies reveal whether the distribution of one variable changes across categories of the other. Expected counts provide a concrete benchmark for the independence hypothesis.

For this illustrative cybersecurity dataset, the chi-square test measures how strongly observed login outcomes deviate from the independence benchmark. Its conclusion is about the evidence in the observed sample, not proof of a population-level relationship or of a causal mechanism.
